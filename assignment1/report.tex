\section{Ping Test and Round-Trip Time}

\subsection{Method}

We tested every destination listed in the provided IP-address file, along with
our own public IP address.  The script sent ten ICMP echo requests to each
destination and recorded the minimum, average, and maximum RTT, using
\texttt{NA} for non-responsive destinations.  It obtained latitude and
longitude from \url{ip-api.com} and calculated great-circle distance with the
Haversine formula using an Earth radius of 6,371 km.

The code used to collect and process the ping measurements is available here:
\codelink{ping collection}{assignment1/part1/ping.sh\#L55-L56} and
\codelink{RTT calculation}{assignment1/part1/ping.sh\#L58-L66}.

\subsection{Discussion}

RTT generally increases with geographic distance because signals need more
time to travel over longer physical paths.  The relationship is not exact,
however, because packets follow routes selected by network providers rather
than a direct geographic path.  Peering locations, indirect routing, and
router processing can all add latency.

The minimum RTT is the best estimate of the path's baseline latency.  It
mostly reflects propagation, transmission, and processing delay with little
temporary queueing.  The maximum RTT is the slowest observed reply and shows
how network conditions varied during the measurement.  Congestion, queueing,
route changes, and ICMP processing priority can increase maximum RTT above the
baseline.  Therefore, distance establishes an important lower bound on RTT,
while routing and short-term network conditions explain much of the variation.

Each destination was traced in ICMP-echo mode with three probes per hop, a
two-second timeout, and a 30-hop limit. The per-hop RTT is the minimum of the
responsive probes. Non-responsive hops are recorded as \texttt{*}, and a
destination that does not respond after a retry is skipped.

\subsection{Discussion}

The stacked bar chart shows that latency is concentrated in a few larger
increments, usually in the long-haul part of the route, rather than being
distributed evenly across hops. Non-responsive hops have no measured RTT and
are omitted from the breakdown.

Hop count has only a weak positive relationship with RTT. Routes with the
same number of hops can have different RTTs because the distance and quality
of their links differ.
